\caption{Robustness of working-memory retrieval to three types of trigger-window perturbation. The trained MNESIS network is triggered by $11$ independent realisations of the perturbed trigger, each drawing the trigger noise and the spontaneous activity from its own random seed. The $F_1$ score is computed on the spikes of each of the $M=16$ motifs. In the plot, each point is the mean over these realisations, and error bars span the $5\%$ to $95\%$ quantiles of a beta distribution fitted to them; and thin blue lines show the $F_1$ score of each motif. \emph{Left:} Bit-flip noise applied to all spikes in the trigger window ($p_\mathrm{flip} \in [0,1]$); the network maintains high $F_1$ up to $p_\mathrm{flip} \approx 0.2$. \emph{Middle:} Truncated trigger window, reduced to its last steps (trigger duration from $1$ to $D = 41$ steps); retrieval remains reliable for durations above roughly $29$ steps. \emph{Right:} Partial neuron coverage (number of silenced neurons from $0$ to $N = 1024$); the $F_1$ score degrades smoothly as more neurons are silenced and drops sharply once the majority of the trigger population is inactive. In each panel, the orange line is the first-spike theory, the $F_1$ score expected when the replay of a motif, a cascade decided by its first evoked spike, starts only if at least a fraction $\vartheta$ of the $\approx p_A N D \approx 6.7$ spikes supporting that spike are present in the perturbed trigger window (Equation~\ref{eq:ignition}, a mean-field model of the pseudo-inverse readout, with the precision and recall of the intact trigger as its only measured inputs), and the green line is the cascade theory, the same model simulated step by step over the whole replay of $1000$ random motifs.}\label{fig:noise}
